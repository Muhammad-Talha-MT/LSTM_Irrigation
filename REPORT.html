LSTM irrigation scheduling

Executed synthetic experiments supporting a five-part technical response

Evidence boundary. These are simulated farms and generated weather. The network learns a known water-balance scheduling rule. Results do not demonstrate field readiness, yield benefits, or superiority over expert irrigation.

What was actually run

39 trained models, three initialization seeds, eight source farms, two unseen target farms, and separate training, validation and test seasons. Each controller is evaluated for all 60 days from April 1 through May 30. Checkpoints, input datasets, every test prediction and every closed-loop daily trajectory are included.

1. Training design

The model sees past sequences and fresh morning observations. Labels come from an explicit teacher. See docs/METHODS.md for the feature dictionary, equations, event timing, split definitions and assumptions.

2. How much data?

Increasing the source training set from one to six synthetic seasons per farm reduced mean MAE from 1.056 to 0.548 mm/day. This does not establish a real-world minimum. Irrigation events are rare: only 55 of 960 ordinary source test days have positive teacher labels. F1 and closed-loop outcomes are essential alongside MAE.

3. Transfer is not automatically beneficial

Head-only adaptation changed target-farm-8 MAE from 1.111 to 1.040 mm/day. On target farm 9 it worsened MAE from 0.504 to 1.272 mm/day. Local validation and an unchanged-model comparison are necessary. Sparse labels make learning from scratch difficult: the target-farm-9 scratch models predicted no positive events on the test teacher trajectories (F1 = 0).

4. A storm changes inputs, not necessarily weights

All ordinary-trained LSTM rollouts recommended zero irrigation on May 1 after the 80 mm overnight event was observed. No May retraining was performed. Across source farms, updating daily reduced May irrigation from 71.607 mm under the stale counterfactual schedule to 41.938 mm. This is an intentionally stale-plan comparison, not a claim of savings against a competent water-balance scheduler.

The simulator drains excess water immediately; it cannot establish safe behavior under persistent waterlogging. A storm arriving after the morning decision would require an intraday rain interlock.

5. Check control outcomes, not only prediction error

The MLP has lower source one-step MAE (0.313 versus 0.548 mm/day), but on the shifted target farms its ordinary-weather closed-loop stress proxy averages 10.333 days versus 0.833 for the unchanged LSTM. These tests ask different questions. The no-irrigation baseline and scratch models show why small water totals alone are not evidence of a good scheduler.

6. Preparing for sudden changes

We tested equal-size ordinary and storm-augmented training datasets. Augmentation did not improve held-out imitation MAE: storm-test MAE was 0.521 versus 0.370 mm/day. Ordinary observations plus measured storage were already sufficient for the simulated storm. This negative result argues for evaluating augmentation, not assuming it must help. The wet-state guard also showed no additional reduction in wet-state irrigation events in the learned rollouts, because neither guarded nor unguarded LSTM produced such events here.

Limitations and next field step

This is a small, single-bucket, early-season experiment with directly observed root-zone moisture and labels generated by a known rule. Training weather and test weather come from the same generator apart from the injected storm and two parameter-shifted farms. It does not test regional climate transfer, damaged drains, yield, prolonged flooding, or learning from imperfect human actions. Farm-level soil profiles, measured irrigation, historical forecasts, calibration and prospective monitored testing are needed before field use.

The proposed response is in docs/PROFESSOR_RESPONSE.md. Methods, assumptions and primary references are in docs/METHODS.md. Run instructions are in README.md.
